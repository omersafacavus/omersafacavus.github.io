## Two disciplines, one toolbox

Additive manufacturing was born in engineering labs, but the same machines (laser powder bed fusion, binder jetting, FDM, resin SLA) are now reshaping how things are designed and made in architecture, sculpture, jewellery, fashion and industrial design. The overlap creates a feedback loop: engineers push material and process boundaries; artists push form and intent. Both sides benefit when they talk.

This page is a bridge. If you come from manufacturing, it points to where creative practice can expand your thinking. If you come from art or design, it shows the engineering fundamentals that unlock new possibilities.

## For engineers → art

Additive manufacturing removes many geometric constraints. What happens when you stop optimising for stiffness-to-weight and start optimising for light, texture, or emotional response?

- **Topology optimisation as aesthetic**: Structures generated for minimal mass often look organic. Architects such as Zaha Hadid Architects and studios like Neri Oxman's Mediated Matter group have turned this into a design language.
- **Lattice and infill as surface**: Gradient lattices aren't just weight-saving; they create translucency, flexibility gradients and visual rhythm.
- **Material gradients**: Multi-material printing and controlled microstructure open the door to parts that change colour, reflectivity or texture across their surface.
- **Parametric design**: The same Grasshopper and C# skills used for engineering optimisation can generate complex decorative geometry, tesselations and responsive facades.

## For artists → engineering

Creative ambition runs into material and process limits. Understanding a few engineering fundamentals prevents failed prints, wasted material and months of trial-and-error.

- **Design for AM**: Overhangs, support structures, minimum wall thickness and build orientation all affect whether a design can actually be produced.
- **Scan strategies**: The path the laser follows determines residual stress, distortion and surface quality. A sculptor working in metal AM benefits from the same knowledge a turbine engineer needs.
- **Material science basics**: Why does 316L stainless steel behave differently from titanium? Why does post-processing matter? A short detour into metallurgy saves costly reprints.
- **Simulation**: Thermal simulation predicts warping before you commit to a build. No need to become an FEA expert, but knowing what simulation can answer is valuable.

## Where they meet

Some of the most interesting work happens at the intersection:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| **Architecture** | 3D-printed concrete facades, metal nodes for free-form structures |
| **Jewellery** | Lost-wax casting replaced by direct metal printing; complexity at no extra cost |
| **Sculpture** | Large-format wire-arc AM, robotic deposition, recycled-material filaments |
| **Fashion** | Flexible TPU lattice garments, printed shoe midsoles, custom orthotics as wearable design |
| **Ceramics** | Binder-jetted porcelain, robocasted clay with algorithmic patterns |
| **Musical instruments** | Printed titanium flutes, lattice acoustic chambers, custom mouthpieces |

## Building two communities

This Atlas is primarily an engineering reference, but the plan is to grow a parallel thread for creative practitioners:

- **Shared glossary**: The same terms (support structure, overhang, sintering) appear in both worlds. The glossary will tag terms relevant to arts.
- **Learning paths**: Dedicated paths for designers and artists, starting from form and intent rather than from process parameters.
- **Funding and residencies**: Artist-in-residence programmes at fabrication labs, design grants, and cross-disciplinary calls.
- **Case studies**: Documented projects that show the full pipeline from concept to finished piece.

If you work at this intersection, or want to, contributions are welcome. Open an issue on the site's repository or reach out directly.
