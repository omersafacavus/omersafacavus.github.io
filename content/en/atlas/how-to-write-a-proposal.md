## From idea to application

A research proposal is a structured argument: it must convince reviewers that your question matters, your method can answer it, and you are the right person to carry it out. Before writing, distil your idea into one sentence — the **core claim** — and test it against three questions:

1. Is there a clear gap or need?
2. Can the work produce measurable outcomes within the funding period?
3. Does it align with the call's objectives?

If any answer is weak, sharpen the idea first.

## Reading the call text

Most unsuccessful proposals fail because they answer a question the call did not ask. Read the full call document — not just the summary — and highlight:

- **Eligibility criteria** — institution type, career stage, consortium rules.
- **Thematic scope** — which topics are in, which are explicitly out.
- **Evaluation criteria and weights** — excellence vs. impact vs. implementation.
- **Formatting rules** — page limits, required sections, font sizes.

Make a short checklist from these; review it after every draft.

## Structural skeleton

Most funding bodies expect a similar backbone. Adapt the headings to the call's template, but keep the logic:

1. **Abstract / Summary** — write last; one paragraph that a non-specialist can follow.
2. **State of the art** — what is known, where the gap is, and why it matters now.
3. **Objectives** — concrete, measurable goals (avoid vague verbs like "explore").
4. **Methodology / Work plan** — what you will do, in what order, with which tools.
5. **Expected results and impact** — deliverables, publications, societal or industrial benefit.
6. **Timeline and milestones** — a Gantt chart or table showing work packages, durations and dependencies.
7. **Budget justification** — link every cost line to a work-package task.
8. **Risk management** — identify the top three risks and your mitigation plan.
9. **Team and expertise** — why this group can deliver; prior results that prove competence.
10. **Ethics and data management** — if applicable, address ethical clearance and open-data plans.

## Evaluation criteria

Reviewers score against the call's criteria. The three most common axes are:

- **Scientific excellence** — originality, soundness of methodology, ambition balanced with feasibility.
- **Impact** — benefit to science, industry, society; dissemination and exploitation plan.
- **Quality of implementation** — work plan coherence, team competence, resource allocation.

Write each section with the relevant criterion in mind. If "impact" is worth 30 % of the score, your impact section needs substance, not a paragraph of vague promises.

## Common mistakes

- **Scope creep** — trying to do everything dilutes the proposal's focus.
- **Missing the call's language** — use the call's own terminology; reviewers look for alignment.
- **Weak work plan** — a list of tasks without dependencies, deliverables or timelines.
- **Copy-paste budget** — costs that don't connect to activities signal poor planning.
- **No preliminary data** — if you have pilot results, include them; they reduce perceived risk.
- **Submitting at the last minute** — administrative errors (wrong format, missing signatures) are rejection causes.
